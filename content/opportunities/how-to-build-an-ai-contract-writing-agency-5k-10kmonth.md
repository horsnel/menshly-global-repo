## Pricing: What to Charge and How to Defend It

Don’t charge by the page. Charge for a defined service, a clear turnaround, and the number of revisions. Your client isn’t paying for words; they’re paying to spend less time getting a usable first draft. Be upfront that AI helps you work faster, but a qualified human must review every document. You’re not selling legal advice, and you shouldn’t present your drafts as attorney-approved.

**Starter — $750 per month**  
For a solo consultant or small business that needs occasional documents. Include two first-draft documents per month, a client intake form, one revision per document, and delivery within five business days. Set a clear scope: for example, straightforward service agreements or NDAs based on client-provided terms—not bespoke legal opinions.

**Growth — $2,000 per month**  
For a business with recurring contract work. Include up to six first drafts per month, two revisions per document, a three-business-day turnaround, and a monthly 30-minute workflow review. Track each request in [Notion](https://notion.so/), and use ChatGPT to organize client-provided details into a consistent draft brief before you write.

**Enterprise — $5,000 per month**  
For teams that need predictable capacity and tighter processes. Include up to 15 first drafts per month, priority turnaround, a shared request tracker, and a monthly process review. Set limits on document types, revisions, and response times in writing. Route legal questions to the client’s attorney; don’t guess.

### Pricing Trick: Sell a Paid Pilot

<div class="accent-box">
Offer a two-week pilot for $500: one document type, one first draft, and one revision. If the client signs a monthly plan within seven days of delivery, credit the $500 toward month one. You get paid to prove your process, and the client gets a low-risk way to test the fit.
</div>

Defend the price with scope, not hype. Show what’s included, what’s excluded, and exactly when the clock starts. If a client wants unlimited revisions, rush delivery, or custom legal research, quote that separately—or decline. One client asking for a $750 plan to cover every document in the company isn’t a pricing opportunity. It’s a scope problem.
